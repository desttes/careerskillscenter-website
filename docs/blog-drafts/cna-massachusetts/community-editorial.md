approved here, backed by 2+ independent people, facts checked against official sources, no usernames, no thread links,

## Candidate decisions

### C1. Skills test is graded on exact steps. Say every step out loud.
- **Verdict: KEEP.** Strongest candidate: 12+ people, 5 threads, mostly 2026, and it explains *why* people fail, which no
  competitor does.
- **Value:** Changes how the reader prepares. A reader paying retake fees ($70 for skills, per the handbook; Data
  Researcher to confirm in the May 2026 handbook) cannot afford to fail because of a habit from work. It also protects
  readers who already work as aides and think they don't need to study. Not on any ranking page.
- **Fit:** Strengthens differentiator 2 (current exam facts). Place it directly under the "Exam day at a glance" box as
  "What people who failed the skills test say." Three tips in plain words: get the official D&S skills list, practice
  each skill with someone reading the steps, say each step out loud as you do it.
- **Balance:** Include the reassuring side in one line: many people who leave the test sure they failed actually pass.
  Do not include "some examiners are stricter" (can't be checked, and it makes the test sound random) or the
  "grievance" detail (other state).
- **Quote: YES.** "How you do stuff at work and how you do stuff when taking the state exam are two different things."
  19 words, everyday English, says the whole lesson. Clearer than our paraphrase would be.
- **Must verify before use (Data Researcher, D&S May 2026 handbook):** missing a critical/bold step fails that skill;
  whether candidates are told to verbalize; hand washing as the first task; whether results show what you missed.
  If the handbook does not say "say steps out loud," write it as a tip from test-takers, not a rule.

### C2. "Free" nursing-home training: a real mix
- **Verdict: KEEP.** It is the honesty check on our own hook. Our angle leads with "you may not have to pay"; this
  candidate keeps that from becoming a sales line.
- **Value:** For a low-income reader, free employer training is the most attractive path and the easiest trap. The
  community adds two things competitors lack: experiences really vary (from no strings, even paid during class, to
  1-2 year commitments with payback threats), and a list of questions to ask before signing.
- **Fit:** Differentiator 1, "3 ways to pay." Put a short "Read the contract first" box under the table. Merge the
  community's questions into the "7 questions" box: Who pays the state exam fee? How long must I stay? What happens if
  I leave early, in writing? What is my pay after I pass? Class days and times? How many days of training on the floor
  after I pass? How many residents per aide?
- **Balance (required):** Show both sides in the same paragraph: "Some people say their nursing home paid for
  everything and they owed nothing. Others signed a contract to stay for a year or two and were told they would have to
  pay the money back if they left." Also keep the point that a place offering free training may be one that has trouble
  keeping staff, so look at the workplace too (links to C3).
- **Quote: NO.** The researcher is right; the best lines name employers or sound like legal advice. Paraphrase.
- **Hard limits:** Never tell readers payback clauses are not enforced or are illegal. The federal no-charge rule
  (42 CFR 483.152(c), confirmed on the Cornell copy) can be stated as written; how it interacts with a "repay if you
  quit" clause is a legal question we do not answer. Say: "If a contract asks you to pay back training, ask DPH or a
  free legal aid office before you sign." Drop the search-summary claim about "12 months" unless the Data Researcher
  finds it in an official source. "Some places pay you during class" may appear only as "some people say," never as a
  promise.

### C3. Pick your first workplace carefully
- **Verdict: KEEP.** 8+ people, 5 threads. Practical and not on any competitor page.
- **Value:** A reader leaving a minimum-wage job can't afford to quit after a week. A short "before you take the job"
  checklist, plus one official lookup tool, prepares them better than any pay figure.
- **Fit:** Adds a small new structural element: a **"Before you take the job" checklist** after the registry/renewal
  section (pairs with the existing "Before you sign up" box). Items: ask how many residents each aide has on each shift
  and how many need full help; ask how long orientation is; be careful if they offer the job on the spot with no
  questions; look the nursing home up on Medicare's Care Compare (star ratings, staffing, inspections).
- **Balance:** Present red flags as "things CNAs say to watch for," not as proof a place is bad. Include the
  counterpoint lightly or not at all; it doesn't change the advice.
- **Quote: NO.** The quote option ("i've found that the faster a place wants to hire you...") cannot be used unaltered
  (lowercase "i"), and we don't edit quotes. Paraphrase: "Several CNAs say that when a place wants to hire you on the
  spot, without many questions, that can be a warning sign."
- **Must verify:** Care Compare on medicare.gov shows nursing home star ratings, staffing and inspection results
  (official source; cite it, not ProPublica). **Drop** the "below 3 stars can't host clinicals" claim. **State no CNA
  ratio** for Massachusetts.

### C4. The first weeks feel overwhelming, and it gets easier
- **Verdict: KEEP.** 10+ people, 5 threads, strong agreement.
- **Value:** Prepares the reader for the hardest moment, the first solo shifts, so normal struggle isn't read as
  personal failure. Competitors stop at "get certified."
- **Fit:** A short "Your first weeks on the job" paragraph inside or right after "Is this job for you?" Tips worth
  keeping: ask for help with any two-person task; ask HR whether a longer orientation is possible; a dementia-only
  (memory care) unit is a hard first job; some people find evening shifts calmer to start. Skip time estimates
  ("3 weeks to a few months"), or say "a few weeks to a few months, people say."
- **Balance:** Include that some people quit after a few days and were glad they did, because the place was unsafe;
  one regretted quitting too fast. The message is "slow at first is normal; unsafe is not."
- **Quote: YES.** "You will be slow at first and that's okay." 9 words, kind, very easy to read. A paraphrase would lose
  the reassurance. Do not attach any claim about how long orientation must be.

### C5. Back injuries and two-person tasks done alone
- **Verdict: KEEP.** 15+ people. Explains *why* the BLS injury warning is true and what a new aide can do.
- **Value:** Turns "high injury rate" into an action: use the lift, wait for a second person, raise the bed to your
  height. That is more useful than a statistic.
- **Fit:** "Is this job for you?" right after the BLS Work Environment line (BLS is the fact; the community is the
  explanation). Strengthens the honesty part of differentiator 3.
- **Balance:** Include that some workplaces enforce lift use and injuries are rare there. This also feeds the C3
  checklist ("ask whether lifts are available on every unit"). Do not describe specific surgeries; "serious back
  injuries" is enough.
- **Quote: NO.** "2 person assist" is jargon and the line lacks punctuation; it would confuse a basic-English reader.
  Paraphrase: "CNAs say most back injuries happen when a job that needs two people gets done by one, because the unit
  is short-staffed."
- **Cut the side tip** about not telling an employer about back pain (borders on advising people to hide health
  information).

### C6. Shift patterns and pressure to pick up extra shifts
- **Verdict: KEEP, short (2-3 sentences or checklist items).**
- **Value:** Working parents need to know the schedule shape before they spend money and time on training.
- **Fit:** "Before you take the job" checklist: ask the shift length (8 or 12 hours), how often you work weekends and
  holidays, and whether nights or weekends pay more. One line: many CNAs say you will be asked often to pick up extra
  shifts, and it's okay to say no.
- **Balance:** Already mixed (some love three 12-hour days; others can't do 12s or nights). Keep both in one sentence.
- **Quote: NO.** Paraphrase. Do not state shift times as a rule ("schedules vary") and state no differential amounts.

### C7. Is it worth it? Split views
- **Verdict: KEEP.** 20+ people, 6 threads; genuinely mixed, which is exactly what our "Is this job for you?" section
  needs.
- **Value:** No competitor gives real voices on both sides. This is where our honesty edge is most visible.
- **Fit:** Opening of "Is this job for you?" One quote, then a plain paraphrase of the hard side, then the BLS facts.
  Useful paraphrase points: purpose and caring for people; jobs are easier to find in nursing homes than in hospitals
  for brand-new CNAs (pair with BLS: 36% work in nursing care facilities, 32% in hospitals); many use it as a step
  toward nursing school; hard on the body; short staffing; dealing with death; pay feels low for the work.
- **Balance (required):** The negative paraphrase must sit right next to the quote, with equal weight. Add one line
  that people often post online to vent, so forums lean negative; that keeps us fair without dismissing the downsides.
- **Quote: YES.** "I say it's my least favorite job, but I'm never doing anything else." 13 words, plain, honest about
  the love-hate mix. Earns its place.
- **Do not use:** hiring bonus amounts, hourly pay from posts, "83 rejections" (one person, identifying detail), or
  "hospitals help pay for nursing school" as a general fact.

### C8. Intimate care feels awkward at first
- **Verdict: KEEP, one sentence.** 5 people agreed (one thread, but 5 independent voices meets the 2-person rule), low
  risk, no facts to verify.
- **Value:** A real worry that stops people from starting and that no guide names. Naming it plainly helps the reader
  decide honestly.
- **Fit:** "Is this job for you?" list, as a plain item: "You will help people use the toilet, bathe and get clean.
  CNAs say it feels awkward at first, then quickly becomes normal. The key is protecting the person's dignity."
- **Balance:** Everyone agreed; no forced counterpoint needed. Keep the tip that you can report residents who act
  inappropriately (a safety note, not just reassurance).
- **Quote: NO.** It's a good line, but it uses "clinical," which our reader may not know, and we are at our quote
  budget. Paraphrase as above.

### C9. Hidden costs if you pay yourself, plus a deposit scam
- **Verdict: KEEP, as questions, never as numbers.** 5 people across 4 threads, all other states.
- **Value:** Low-income readers need the whole price before starting. Turning this into "ask what the price includes"
  questions avoids needing any Massachusetts cost data (which the research brief could not find from a neutral source).
- **Fit:** "3 ways to pay," pay-yourself row, and the "7 questions" box: "Does the price include the state exam, books,
  uniform, CPR card, TB test and shots, and background check?" Add one safety line: check that the program is on DPH's
  approved list before you pay anything, and be careful with programs that push you to pay a deposit fast.
- **Balance:** Not needed beyond "costs vary a lot by school."
- **Quote: NO.**
- **Must verify / limits:** Do not say Massachusetts programs *require* TB tests, shots or CPR unless DPH says so; as
  "ask whether the price includes..." it makes no claim. SNAP Path to Work (DTA) as a CNA funding route: **leave out**
  unless the Data Researcher confirms it on mass.gov; if confirmed, it is one line in the public-programs row. No
  California dollar amounts.

### C10. Studying with a job, kids or English as a second language
- **Verdict: MAYBE (keep 2-3 tips).** Only 4 people in 2 threads, and the most important part (is basic English

- **Value:** Simple study habits help our working-parent and ESL readers. The open question itself is useful: the

  That is an honest statement we can make without forum support.
- **Fit:** Short tip list after C1 in the exam section: study in short pieces, use flashcards and practice tests,
  practice skills on a family member. Then the honest English line in the languages paragraph, plus "ask the program
  if it has an ESL track" (already in the strategy's questions box).
- **Balance:** Not applicable.
- **Quote: NO.**
- **Must not state:** any dictionary rule for Massachusetts (California rule only) and any required English level,
  unless D&S or DPH says so.

### C11. Reciprocity online through D&S

## Effect on the angle and hook

**Angle: no change.** The money-first, no-sales, 2026-current angle stays. The community material strengthens it in two
places:
1. **Differentiator 1 (3 ways to pay)** gets more honest: C2 shows that "free" can come with strings, and C9 turns
   "pay yourself" into a concrete checklist.
2. **Differentiator 3 (barriers and honest job picture)** gets real voices: C4, C5, C7 and C8 make "Is this job for you?"
   the section no competitor has.

**Recommended additions to the structure (writer):**
- After the "Exam day at a glance" box: **"Why people fail the skills test"** (C1, with C10 tips).
- New **"Before you take the job" checklist** (C3, C6, C5 lift question), separate from the "Before you sign up"
  training box. This is a small but distinct new differentiator: competitors stop at certification; we help the reader
  survive the first job.

**Hook: one adjustment.** Keep opening on the federal no-charge rule, but add a balancing clause in the first paragraph
so the hook doesn't oversell "free." Direction: "...it cannot charge you for that training. Some of these free programs
come with a work contract, so we also show you what to ask before you sign." This keeps the hook true to C2 and to our
honesty promise. Do not put any community quote in the hook; the C1, C4 and C7 quotes belong in the body.
